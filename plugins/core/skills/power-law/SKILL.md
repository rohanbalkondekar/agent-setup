# Power Law — leverage razors and workplace power check

Two modes. Pick by input, say which you're running:

- **Rank** — the input is a set of things the user *could* do (backlog, to-do list, options, "where should I help with X"). Find the vital few, name the trivial many to drop, reframe at least one item as a 10x move. Output decisions, not philosophy.
- **Power-check** — the input is a communication or situation (draft message, email, meeting plan, political read). Run the 48 laws + strategy lenses; flag what fires, fix it, rewrite the draft.

A draft about *choosing between options* may need both: power-check the wording, rank the underlying options.

## Rank mode

### Procedure

1. **Enumerate** the full option set. If the user gave a vague area ("how can I help with X?"), first gather real state (tickets, docs, messages) so you rank actual options, not hypotheticals. Nothing useful happens until the items are real and named.
2. **Define the outcome variable.** Leverage is meaningless without "toward what": career capital, project success, revenue, visibility, learning. Ask only if genuinely ambiguous; otherwise infer and state your assumption.
3. **Quadrant each item (Eisenhower).** Urgent = demands attention now (deadline, someone waiting). Important = moves a real goal forward. They are not the same — most stress comes from treating urgent as important.
   - **Q1 urgent + important → do now, yourself.** Real fires. If the *whole* list is Q1, they're firefighting — the cure is Q2.
   - **Q2 important, not urgent → schedule it.** Highest-value, most-skipped quadrant: planning, deep work, prevention, learning, relationships. Crowded out until it becomes a Q1 emergency. Protect a block.
   - **Q3 urgent, not important → delegate or defer.** Loud but not theirs to do. Hand off, batch, or "after 3pm". Masquerades as Q1 — say *why* it isn't when non-obvious.
   - **Q4 neither → delete.** Busywork, over-polishing. Stop.
4. **Score within quadrants against the razors** below. Don't score theatrically (no fake numbers); sort into three buckets:
   - **Vital few (~top 20%)** — disproportionate outcome share; do these.
   - **Middle** — real but linear payoff; delegate, batch, or timebox.
   - **Trivial many** — effort ≈ outcome or worse; explicitly kill or ignore. Naming the kill-list is mandatory — a prioritization that drops nothing is a failure.
5. **10x reframe:** pick at least one item and ask what the 10x version looks like. 10x beats 2x because 2x competes with everyone doing the default thing harder; 10x forces a different method, and different methods are usually *less* crowded, not more. If the 10x version is unreachable, say so and why.
6. **Deliver:** ranked list with one-line "why" each, the kill-list, the 10x reframe, and the single next action for the #1 item. Name the meta-insight when it applies: an all-Q1 day means too little Q2 last week — coach toward more Q2 so less explodes into Q1.

### Coaching rules

- Apply to *their* situation. A generic framework lecture is a failure; the value is in placing *their* tasks and handing back *their* next action.
- Recommend, don't dictate. Surface the placement and the tradeoff; the user owns the call.
- One clear next step beats a perfect plan. End with "start here."
- If they're overwhelmed, fewer words. Sort the list, name the first move, stop.

### The razors

- **Pareto / power law:** outcomes across options are power-law distributed — a few options carry most of the value. Corollary (Pareto squared): 20% of the 20% → ~4% of inputs give ~64% of output. Always ask "what's the 20% of the 20%?"
- **10x > 2x (Sullivan/Hardy, Teller):** 2x goals are achieved by doing more of the same; 10x goals can only be achieved by changing the method — which eliminates most of the task list automatically. 10x filters harder than it demands.
- **Theory of constraints (Goldratt):** the system's output is set by its bottleneck. Work anywhere else is invisible. Identify the constraint before ranking anything.
- **Amdahl's law:** improving a part caps out at that part's share of the whole. A 90% speedup of a 5% component is noise.
- **Leverage hierarchy (Naval):** labor < capital < code/media (permissionless, zero marginal cost). Prefer work that runs while you sleep: tools, docs, automations, published evidence.
- **Compounding:** prefer assets over tasks — things that accumulate (reputation, reusable systems, written-down evidence, relationships) beat things that evaporate (meetings attended, tickets closed).
- **Asymmetry / barbell (Taleb):** favor bets with capped downside and uncapped upside. A cheap experiment that might change the org's direction outranks a safe task that can't. Never accept unbounded downside (open-ended ownership, standing obligations) for a bounded gain.
- **Hamming question:** "What are the most important problems in your field, and why aren't you working on them?" If the top-ranked item isn't one of the important problems, re-examine the ranking.
- **Opportunity cost / Hell-yeah-or-no (Sivers):** every yes is a no to the vital few. Mediocre opportunities are the expensive ones because they consume vital-few capacity.
- **One decision > 100 decisions:** a policy, automation, or standard that removes a recurring class of decisions beats resolving instances one at a time.
- **Skin in the game / proximity to the outcome:** work whose result is visibly attributable to you compounds career capital; invisible glue work is real but power-law-poor unless made visible (write it up).

### Anti-patterns to flag

- **Peanut-buttering:** spreading effort evenly across all options. The whole point of the power law is that even allocation is maximally wrong.
- **Bottleneck blindness:** polishing a non-constraint because it's tractable or fun.
- **2x-by-default:** framing every improvement as "do the current thing better." Ask for the method change.
- **Urgency capture:** ranking by loudness/recency instead of outcome share (urgent ≠ important).
- **Unkilled zombies:** prioritized lists where nothing was dropped.

### Rank output format

Keep it tight:

1. **Assumed outcome variable** (one line).
2. **Constraint** (one line — what actually gates the system).
3. **Quadrant summary** (one line per non-empty quadrant).
4. **Vital few** — ranked, one-line why + next action each.
5. **Kill / ignore list** — with one-line permission-to-drop rationale.
6. **10x reframe** — one item, the method change, what it would obsolete.

## Power-check mode

Reviewing a workplace communication or situation through the 48 Laws of Power. The goal is practical: help the user communicate effectively, protect themselves politically, and avoid unforced errors. This is NOT a manipulation toolkit.

### Ethical stance (non-negotiable)

- Use the laws **defensively** (spot when others may be playing them on the user) and **constructively** (clarity, timing, framing, reputation).
- Never advise deception, taking credit for others' work, sabotage, or exploiting people. When a law's literal advice is unethical, reframe it (each law in the reference has a work-safe reading) or flag it only as "watch for others doing this to you."
- If the user's draft itself is manipulative or dishonest, flag that as an issue too.

### Procedure

1. **Get the input.** If the user hasn't provided the draft/situation, ask for: the text (or situation), the audience (who, their seniority/relationship), and the goal.
2. **Read [references/48-laws.md](references/48-laws.md) and run ALL 48 laws, one by one, in order.** For each law, silently ask: does this law fire here?
3. **Report only the laws that fire.** Do not pad the report with 48 entries — most laws won't apply to a given input. For each flagged law:
   - `Law N — Name` and whether it's a **Violation**, **Risk** (others may play this on you), or **Opportunity**
   - One or two sentences: what in the input triggers it
   - A concrete fix: the rewritten sentence, the thing to cut, or the action to take
4. **Run the strategy lenses** (below) on the ACT itself — is this the right move, at the right time, at the right scale — not just the wording.
5. **Verdict.** End with: overall read in 1–2 sentences, the top 3 changes ranked, and — if the input was a draft — a full revised version incorporating the fixes.
6. If genuinely nothing fires, say so plainly and note the 1–2 laws that came closest.

Severity ordering in the report: Violations first, then Risks, then Opportunities.

### Strategy lenses (run on every power-check, after the laws)

The laws check HOW the move is made. These check WHETHER it's the right move at all. Report findings under a `### Strategy lenses` heading between Opportunities and Verdict; skip any lens that genuinely doesn't apply.

**Eisenhower** — classify the act itself using the quadrants from Rank mode. Check: is the user about to spend prime energy on a Q3 task because it feels urgent? Is a Q2 act (relationship-building, system-building, the one-pager that kills a class of asks) being deferred again?

**80/20 and the power law** — apply the razors above to the act. What is the leverage per keystroke — does a 30-second message unlock four downstream items, or does an hour of effort move nothing? When comparing options, weigh the single dominant outcome (best or catastrophic) rather than averaging: which branch, if it happens, dwarfs everything else? Never negotiate away a position to protect a low-probability upside.

**10x vs 2x** — is the user optimizing a thing that should be replaced (automating a queue nobody should own; polishing a manual process that needs a system)? Does this act commit them to linear effort scaling (every new request costs them time) instead of building the thing that makes the requests self-serve? If the honest answer to "what would the 10x version be?" is a different act entirely, say so.

**Boss-axis (make the master successful)** — the relationship with the boss, and each senior stakeholder whose judgment matters, is the single most important career asset; it compounds across roles and companies for 20–30 years. The unit of value is not "task done well"; it is the delta this act makes on the axes the boss is judged on. Checks:

1. **Name the bosses in this situation.** The direct manager, plus any senior stakeholder whose read of the user this act will shape. Not just the recipient.
2. **Map their axes.** For each: how do they look to THEIR boss? What is their promotion or credibility riding on right now? Which of their reports or projects is causing them pain? What are their current sources of stress (a KPI, a deadline, a postmortem, a headcount target)? If the user cannot answer these, that IS the finding — the fix is to go find out before optimizing wording.
3. **Score the act against those axes.** Does this message/plan visibly move a number the boss is measured on, remove a stress, or make them look good upward? Or is it correct-but-orthogonal — polished work that changes nothing they care about? Tie the ask or result explicitly to their axis, in their metric's language (pairs with Laws 13 and 44).
4. **Stress direction.** Does this act hand the boss a new problem, an open question, or homework — or does it arrive with the work absorbed and a decision that costs one word to approve? Prefer subtracting from their load.
5. **Relationship compounding.** Bosses, companies, and roles change; the relationships remain. Does this act invest in the long relationship (learning from them, making them successful, being the person they'd hire again) or spend it for a short-term point?
6. **The trap.** Flag any smell of "my job is just my tasks" — the user completing assignments cleanly while ignoring what the people above them are actually judged on. This is the default failure state; awareness decays back to it under load.

**The five career failure modes** — talented people stall for five reasons. Check the user's stance against each — both that they're not falling into one, and (defensively) whether a counterpart is:

1. **Refusal to believe / fear of failure.** "I'm just a cog; no one will listen; if I do this I'll upset somebody." Check: is the user shrinking the ask, pre-surrendering, or not sending the message at all because they've decided the answer for the other person?
2. **Lack of purpose.** Fighting for inconsequential or purely selfish goals ("will this get me promoted?"). Check: is this act tied to something the user actually believes matters, or is it point-scoring? If the user can't say what the fight is FOR, flag it.
3. **Lack of self-awareness.** "Why doesn't the CEO like my view on company strategy?" Check: is the ask calibrated to the user's actual standing and track record with this audience? Are they spending influence they haven't earned yet?
4. **Refusal to play.** "These guys just do what they want; they won't listen." A worldview is only as powerful as the ability to enroll others in it. Check: is the user avoiding a stakeholder because that person doesn't share their worldview — the exact person they need to persuade?
5. **Refusal to strategize or concede.** Unwillingness to win a side quest that furthers the goal because the interim step feels pointless, or inability to concede a battle to win the war. Check: is the user fighting a small wrong thing at the cost of the big right thing? Is there a side quest (a demo, a small favor, an interim deliverable) that would buy the real objective?

The mindset behind the lens: believe in something worth doing against the odds, know why it's important, know what needs to be done to make it real, do it, and take the side quests that get it done. Almost everyone bitter or stuck traces to one of the five above — most commonly 1, 2, and 3, and modes 2–5 eventually collapse into 1. Flag drafts and plans that carry the smell of any of them.

### Hard stop

If the situation touches HR, legal, compliance, security incidents, safety, harassment, or formal performance/disciplinary processes: skip the 48-laws lens entirely. There, the only advice is full candor, required disclosure, a preserved written record, and escalation to the proper owner — never strategic omission or timing games.
